# Read a mathematical method paper

Start from the requested source and fix its version. Record every heading and source location in the route. Use `japanese-translation` for the continuous Japanese text. Preserve the abstract, statements, equations, algorithms and references in source order. Place source corrections beside the affected passage as notes.

Produce 忠実訳 and 通読（詳読つき） from one source-aligned Japanese text, followed by appendices in the way a method paper is laid out: 付録 A 証明, 付録 B 実装と対応, 付録 C 検算 and 付録 D 資料. Keep reading and writing in one system: the paper-order skeleton is the `writing-harness` outline (`structure.yaml`), the same cards serve a reader and a future manuscript, and the pages look the same for both. In 忠実訳, keep the translated captions, source references and original figures on demand. Make 通読 the main text of that paper: the translation read top to bottom, with the figures in place and, after the sentence, equation or algorithm step they belong to, at most a few numbered key statements per section (命題 4.7, 定義 2.1). Mark algorithm steps with ▷ and the numbers of the statements and Appendix B steps they rely on. Put the detailed reading of every passage, all other statements and every proof in Appendix A, and the steps from statements to computation in Appendix B, numbered (補題 A.12, B.16.3) and in paper order. In all three, let each source section or subsection fold under its heading and fold nothing deeper; the main text starts open, the appendices closed, and a link opens its target's section.

Write each long text once: the key statement and its point in the main text, the proof in Appendix A, the computation in Appendix B. Elsewhere give only a short guide that links to it: after a key statement its proof outline (the point) and the code flow (the titles of its Appendix B steps in order); at the head of each Appendix A passage its code flow; at the head of each Appendix B step the proof outlines of the statements it uses. Do not repeat the intent in a separate box. Use Appendix C for additional figures, independent diagnostics and hand calculations, and place translated implementations such as R or Python beside the code they port in Appendix B.

Add 原文画像 links under each heading in 通読（詳読つき） when the local source is a PDF; each link opens one cropped region by itself. Record the version-pinned PDF regions in reading order. For a passage that crosses pages or columns, include each continuation and label it with its physical PDF page. Check the first heading, the last line and figures or equations at the joins. Keep page facsimiles local and omit them from 忠実訳.

Use descriptive titles and factual sentences in the detailed reading. State quantities, assumptions and conclusions explicitly. Show what is plotted, where an observation is used again, and how a formula or operation appears in the code. Keep judgments about what matters and optional hand exercises in separate reading notes. Preserve the attribution of any question supplied by the reader.

Keep shared evidence in cards and let the source map supply the reading order and links.

- Read the source and the companion one mathematical assertion at a time. Inventory definitions, each separately provable claim, formula transformations, observations from figures and runs, and what each substantive code operation computes. Leave connective prose in the reading. Split an assertion when its assumptions, justification or scope differ. Write the setting, precise assertion and reason for each item, and identify which earlier statements it uses. Show them in Appendix A under the passage's reading, each with its number, and keep the title readable before the reason.
- Cover every paragraph of the source. Each paragraph is either read in a passage or recorded in the paragraph ledger with the reason it holds no claim (introduction, transition, algorithm heading, history, related work, the authors' evaluation) or with the passage that handles its claim. Sweep especially for sentences that look like commentary but assert something: complexity ("O(n³)", "the bulk of the cost", "cannot be improved"), rates and consistency ("converges", "all estimators converge to d"), impossibility and necessity ("cannot", "requires"), frequency words ("usually", "often") and results attributed to cited work. Record each paragraph's fingerprint so that a change in the translation reopens it.
- Turn each such sentence into a statement with its hypotheses. For complexity, use kind `cost` and write the model: which operation counts as a step, the input sizes, the algorithm chosen, and whether the bound is upper, lower or tight; separate what follows from the chosen algorithm from what holds for the method itself. When the source drops a hypothesis, state the statement with it and say what fails without it (for example, the Θ(n²) memory of Isomap holds only while the full distance matrix is stored).
- Treat the original work as part of the reading. For every statement that restates a cited result, or that the source asserts without proof, record `origin`: the source card or citation, the locator, and how the source's wording compares with the original (agrees, agrees once hypotheses are added, stronger than the original, weaker, contradicts, or unchecked), with a note on any difference. Read the cited passage when it is available under the intake rules. When the original is paywalled, or the source gives no citation, look for an openly accessible substitute that states the same result and cites the original (a survey, a later paper, the library's code), record it as the `src` with `substitute` explaining why the original was replaced, and say so in the note; do the same for a code card's lineage. Only when no substitute exists, mark it unchecked and keep the statement `open`. Record errors in the source's own text (such as a mistyped matrix) as a source note beside the passage. Use kind `cited` for statements whose support is the cited work rather than a proof here.
- Give each definition and proposition a short `point`: which hypothesis, step or formula does the work, in one or two factual sentences. Mark the key statements of each section with `main: true`: the statement the section's method rests on, such as the objective that becomes an eigenproblem, and the statement that fixes the guarantee or limitation the section discusses. Allow at most `max_main_per_section` (default two) and none for overview sections without such a statement. In 通読 show only these, with their point. Write the point so that it can stand as the outline of the proof (for a definition, as its role). In Appendix A, open a key statement with a link to the main text and any other item with its point as one or two plain sentences, then the statement, the premises used, the proof and a short line of where the result is used (computed from `requires` and walkthroughs).
- Write the definition or proposition as a continuous textbook statement in the reader. Introduce the ambient sets, spaces, maps and indexed families in mathematical prose, then state the hypotheses, quantifier order and conclusion. Use displayed equations where they carry the result and keep short relations inline. Introduce each local symbol where it first becomes necessary. Give quantifier scope and the dependence of existence constants even when the quantifiers are written in words. Match the level of detail to relevant local book cards and lecture examples, including the writing corpus if available. Store the equivalent closed formula for the harness to check and compare it with the statement. Keep the proof or supporting argument after the statement. See [the statement and formula format](references/harness.md#statements-in-textbook-form).
- Record qualitative figure observations with their source and panel. When an observation admits an exact formulation, bind the sample, index and measured quantity. For a code correspondence, state a relation between explicitly bound inputs, intermediate values or outputs, and cite the implementing lines.
- Before creating mathematical cards, search the configured book-harness pools. Compare hypotheses, conclusions and notation. Reuse a matching card by ID, owning route and `book borrow` version. Render the card's own definition or statement at its point of use in the passage's reading in Appendix A, followed by the owning route's proof. Include further cards where a prerequisite is needed to follow the argument on the same page. In the method passage explain the particular substitution or extra assumption and how it reaches the formula, figure or code. Keep book cards as the canonical text. Keep originals, OCR and page images in local storage outside every Git, including local history.
- For gaps, use the literature intake procedure in `reading-reference`. Start with already confirmed copies and the original publisher, author or official repository. Adopt new external resources only when they are openly accessible and their terms allow the intended use. Exclude paywalled, closed, login-gated, provenance-uncertain or otherwise restricted resources and record needed gaps. Free viewing alone does not establish reuse permission. Record the exact locator, version, license, terms URL and date checked. Short, necessary derivations may be proposed with their source premises and labelled as local derivations.
- Use `passages` on a `map` card. In `reading`, interleave factual prose, named figure panels, mathematical results, exact code ranges and recorded diagnostics in the order they are needed. Use a short sentence to connect the evidence at a point where the reader would otherwise have to infer a relationship. Link forward or back when the same figure is used in another passage. See [the harness reference](references/harness.md).
- Add a code walkthrough where the paper uses the implementation. In 通読 give only the code flow. In Appendix B, follow the paper's sections; number each code card used in a section B.k and its steps B.k.j. Give each step a correspondence table (`realization`): for every replacement of a mathematical quantity by a computed one, record both sides, the kind (equality, approximation with its error, numerical computation, or choice), the hypotheses the code satisfies and what lies outside the theorem. Keep steps in execution order and attach the exact source ranges. Write each step in this order: proof outlines of the statements used (by number; statements and proofs stay in Appendix A), code claim, correspondence table, proof of the connection, and actual code. List every correspondence that is not an equality at the end of Appendix B. First state what the lines do under their input conditions, such as the relation between the values before and after an update. Then prove how that relation becomes the mathematical object or hypothesis used by the proposition. Give the mapping of indices, variables and coordinate conventions, and distinguish conditions imposed by the implementation from those used by the mathematics. State and prove as many mathematical claims as the step needs, in dependency order. Identify reader derivations, finite numerical checks, heuristic choices and any unfinished proof obligations. See [the code walkthrough format](references/harness.md#code-walkthroughs).
- Give every code card its lineage (`lineage`): the prior work that proposed the step, with source card and locator; what its authors say the step is for, paraphrased from that passage; whose code this is (the proposers', a public library, an existing local computation, or AI-written reading code) and what it follows; and how it departs from the proposal (defaults, omitted steps, regularization, stopping rules). Mark the record `open` when the proposer's words have not been checked in the primary source. Appendix B opens each code card with this lineage, and Appendix D tables it by proposing source. See [the lineage format](references/harness.md#lineage-of-code-cards).
- To collect the references for a lineage, when reading or when writing a method paper, start from each code step and work backwards: find the paper that introduced the step (not only the survey that cites it), the passage where its authors explain the choice, and the reference implementation it came from (authors' release, official library and version, or a mirror whose identity with the official release is unchecked). Use the intake in `reading-reference`; adopt only openly accessible sources with clear terms, store them locally, and record the version and locator in a source card. When writing, cite the proposing paper for each Algorithm line and state every departure in Appendix B as a `choice` or `approximation` row.
- Store reusable results in `writing-harness` fact cards. When a design argument needs several claims, state the intermediate lemmas and final proposition in dependency order. For each lemma, show where its conclusion is used and which assumptions carry forward. Put local steps in the same derivation and give independently reusable results their own linked cards. Give the precise code ranges when a result is used by the implementation. Add another derivation when it changes the code, the assumptions, the error, or the time or memory cost. Compare the costs with their input parameters and distinguish measured runs from theoretical bounds.
- Use a toy calculation for a finite identity or failure before interpreting a data figure. Record actual executions with their inputs and versions. Give source figures and unexecuted code their own provenance. For a large-data approximation, document what is preserved, the assumptions needed, and what a finite diagnostic can establish.
- When a result may be reused, record its owner, locator, version and applicable assumptions. Keep a possible extension as a question until its new obligations have evidence.

Run the harness checks and rebuild the full reader. Compare both Japanese views against the same source-aligned text. Verify source order against the paper-order outline, paragraph coverage with current fingerprints, the origin of every cited statement, that the main text and Appendices A and B fold only by section, the numbering of key statements in text order, the proof outlines and code flows, original-image coverage, captions, lead, statement and proof of every item in Appendix A, the parts and correspondence rows of every step in Appendix B and the list outside the theory, code ranges and current run records. Read each connection to check that the coding claim implies the claimed mathematical correspondence. Apply `exposition-review` to substantial explanatory revisions. Report the covered source scope and open mathematical checks.

For a whole reader, review the statement inventory against the source passages, captions and implementation. Enable `[reader].require_code_walkthroughs`, `require_points` and `require_realizations` to check that every code reference has a walkthrough with a correspondence table and every definition and proposition has a point. Record the scope of any unfinished justification beside the statement. Verify coverage of passage IDs, the order of prerequisites and the evidence targets. Judge substantive omissions by reading, including statements buried in prose and prerequisites supplied by the companion.

Keep text in step with code and runs in both directions. A change to a statement or proof makes the walkthroughs that use it `STALE-PASSAGE`; a change to code or to a run's result makes the passages that cite it `STALE`. For prose and facts in a `writing-harness`, list the cited code, runs and figures as `evidence` (`ns:id`) and declare `[evidence.<ns>]` with this harness's `fingerprints` command, so that the manuscript is marked STALE as well.

When the same harness serves writing a method paper, keep this layout: the author's prose and the key theorems in the main sections of `structure.yaml`, lemmas in the appendix sections, an Algorithm block whose lines cite the theorems and Appendix B steps, Appendix B generated from the steps' correspondence rows, and Appendix C from runs with known answers. The reader's ▷ markers and the B.k.j steps are the reading form of that Algorithm block and its appendix.
